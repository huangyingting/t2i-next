PHOTOGRAPHIC TRANSLATION OF PRINT LANGUAGE

Translate nishiki-e design principles into physical photography rather than
simulating a printed surface. Create assertive contour separation with dark
textile edges, precise blocking, and controlled rim light; translate flat color
fields into saturated practical set planes; translate bokashi into smooth
in-camera light falloff and atmospheric gradients; translate mica shimmer into
restrained mineral pigment, lacquer, metallic thread, and powdered-gold
highlights on real objects. Use indigo, saffron, persimmon, tea brown, soft
rose, malachite, and warm cream as a limited art-directed palette.

Preserve the strongest graphic qualities of ukiyo-e through flattened spatial
stacking, cropped figures, patterned negative space, rhythmic diagonals,
overlapping textiles, and expressive hands and faces. The illusion may look
strikingly print-like at thumbnail size, but close inspection must reveal
three-dimensional real people, natural skin, woven fibers, individual hairs,
lacquer reflections, paper translucency, tatami texture, and optical depth.
Never add carved outlines, halftones, ink bleed, printed paper grain,
misregistration, foxing, deckled borders, or a scanned-sheet appearance.

Choose a coherent photographic format for each Theme: intimate horizontal
editorial frame, balanced 4:5 portrait, tall full-body fashion portrait, or
another deliberate single-image proportion. Fill the image with the staged

sheet, print border, or photograph of a historical image.

Do not render readable Japanese, pseudo-calligraphy, subtitles, speech
balloons, modern labels, signatures, publisher marks, or imitation artist

blank physical paper panel or an unlettered patch of negative space within the
