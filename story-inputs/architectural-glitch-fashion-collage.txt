BRIEF

Create varied, gallery-caliber architectural glitch fashion photomontages.
Every Theme combines an unmistakably mature adult fashion subject with a
specific architectural world through controlled collage, geometric
displacement, print-registration error, layered transparency, and selective
digital interference. The result is one resolved full-bleed editorial artwork,
not a moodboard, contact sheet, UI screenshot, tutorial, or random collection
of effects.

The reference establishes only a visual grammar: elegant fashion portraiture,
historic and modern architecture, spatial interruption, hard-edged color
blocks, fragmented spatial layers, distressed print surfaces, and a restrained
teal-rust-cream atmosphere. Do not reproduce its people, exact landmarks,
panel arrangement, clothing, colors, facial crop, or glitch placement.

CORE VISUAL IDENTITY

Every Frame must visibly contain all of the following:

1. one dominant mature-adult fashion portrait with a stable identity and
   anatomically coherent face and body;
2. one coherent architectural subject that is more specific than a generic
   city background;
3. meaningful crossings where architecture, portrait, garment, shadow, or
   graphic structure enters another layer;
4. a confident collage gesture supported by a small family of related
   photographic, print, or geometric effects;
5. a restrained editorial palette, tactile surface treatment, and deliberate
   visual hierarchy; and
6. enough unaffected photographic information that the person, architecture,
   clothing, and intended composition remain immediately legible.

The portrait and architecture must transform one another. Do not place an
ordinary portrait over an unrelated building photograph and call it a collage.
Architectural lines may extend into hair, garments, shadow, or negative space;
facial planes may become translucent windows into masonry, steel, ornament,
scaffolding, domes, arches, or interior structure. The fusion must create one
new visual object while preserving human anatomy and architectural identity.

ARTISTIC DIRECTION AND INFLUENCE PALETTE

Treat the image as experimental fashion art rather than a technical glitch
demonstration. For each Theme, combine two or three compatible visual lineages
from the palette below. Use their ways of seeing, cutting, lighting, layering,
and organizing space; do not reproduce a known artwork or recognizable
composition.

- Hannah Höch: incisive Dada photomontage, reassembled portrait fragments,
  surprising scale changes, and visible cut logic;
- El Lissitzky: Proun-like architectural planes, dynamic diagonals, floating
  geometry, and disciplined red-black-cream tension;
- Alexander Rodchenko: radical crop, steep viewpoint, constructivist rhythm,
  bold diagonals, and graphic spatial compression;
- László Moholy-Nagy: translucent light-space, photogram luminosity,
  overlapping planes, and machine-age clarity;
- Erwin Blumenfeld: experimental fashion portraiture, elegant solarization,
  double exposure, veiled faces, mirrors, and sophisticated optical rupture;
- Herbert Bayer: Bauhaus exhibition montage, layered spatial diagrams, clean
  geometric fields, and architecture treated as editorial structure;
- Robert Rauschenberg: transferred photographs, rubbed or abraded surfaces,
  silkscreen layering, and images that bleed materially into one another;
- Eduardo Paolozzi: screenprinted machine fragments, pop-era repetition,
  offset color, and dense but controlled visual sampling;
- Nam June Paik: analog video scan rhythm, signal displacement, and luminous
  electronic color used sparingly;
- Lebbeus Woods: fractured architectural space, tilted structural shards, and
  speculative geometry that still feels materially buildable;
- Deborah Turbeville: faded atmospheric fashion, distressed interiors,
  suspended time, soft grain, and psychologically charged negative space; and
- Guy Bourdin: theatrical fashion staging, saturated accent color, graphic
  body placement, and precise visual tension.

Useful combinations include Höch + Blumenfeld + Lissitzky for elegant facial
reassembly; Moholy-Nagy + Turbeville + Woods for translucent atmospheric
architecture; Rauschenberg + Paolozzi + Bourdin for tactile print collision;
and Bayer + Rodchenko + Paik for graphic spatial montage with analog signal
energy. These are starting points, not fixed recipes.

Let the selected lineage determine the main visual gesture. Höch may suggest
offset cutout anatomy and startling scale shifts; Blumenfeld may suggest
solarization, mirrors, veils, or optical doubling; Moholy-Nagy may suggest
luminous transparency and photogram space; Rauschenberg may suggest rubbed
image transfer and material overlap; Lissitzky or Rodchenko may suggest
diagonal architectural planes; and Paik may suggest horizontal scan
displacement. Do not translate every lineage into the same band across the
eyes.

The collage should be immediately felt, but its strength may come from one
audacious cut, a face interrupted by architecture, a displaced room, a
solarized fashion silhouette, impossible transparency, or a rhythmic cluster
of fragments. Favor expressive visual conviction over counting effects.

Torn paper, grain, scratches, and cracked plaster may enrich the surface but
should not become the entire idea. Avoid reducing the look to scrapbook strips,
a transparent color wash, architectural tattoo, patterned skin, cosmetic eye
mask, or makeup stripe.

FORMAT AND COMPOSITION

Each final Frame is one complete portrait-oriented artwork, preferably 4:5,
3:4, or 2:3. A Theme with multiple Frames may make the Frames function as a
coordinated triptych when viewed together, but each Frame must remain a
self-contained finished image. Do not render three phone screenshots, social
media chrome, playback buttons, interface icons, borders, or separate poster
mockups inside the image.

Choose one primary composition family per Frame:

- centered icon: a frontal or near-frontal portrait aligned with a dome,
  arch, tower, portal, or central vanishing point;
- offset vertical tension: the person occupies one third while architecture
  rises through the opposite two thirds;
- split-axis portrait: face and building follow different vertical axes and
  meet through one decisive horizontal interruption;
- diagonal collision: a Dutch angle, staircase, roofline, scaffold, or shadow
  drives portrait and architecture into one controlled diagonal;
- monumental close crop: face, hat, hair, lips, hands, or jewelry dominate
  while architectural fragments establish scale and place;
- long-body fashion silhouette: a standing, seated, or leaning figure becomes
  a dark vertical counterweight to ornate or industrial structure;
- frame-within-frame: windows, arches, doors, scaffolds, curtains, or graphic
  rectangles enclose selected facial and architectural zones;
- reflected architecture: one credible glass, polished stone, metal, or mirror
  reflection adds a second spatial layer without duplicating the subject;
- stacked strata: foreground portrait, midground geometric interference, and
  background architecture form three readable depth bands; or
- asymmetric negative space: a quiet field of plaster, sky, paper, fog, or
  shadow balances one dense zone of face, architecture, and glitch.

Vary frontal, profile, rear three-quarter, low-angle, elevated oblique,
overhead fragment, eye-level, telephoto compression, and environmental-wide
viewpoints across a batch. Camera roll may use a deliberate 5-20 degree Dutch
angle but never an accidental-looking tilt. Use rectilinear 35-105mm
equivalent perspective; avoid fisheye, peephole distortion, stretched heads,
broken necks, duplicated facial features, or impossible architecture.

PORTRAIT LEGIBILITY AND OCCLUSION

The face may be partially interrupted, but not erased. Preserve enough of the
eye shape, nose bridge, lips, jawline, cheekbone, hairline, or ear for one
recognizable adult identity. A cut may cross the eyes, forehead, mouth, or
lower face, but should not obscure every defining feature at once.

Controlled facial treatments include:

- one horizontal architectural band crossing the eyes or brow;
- a translucent facade aligned with one cheek and temple;
- a displaced rectangular crop shifting one eye or lip by a small, deliberate
  amount while the original facial structure remains inferable;
- a clean vertical seam dividing photographic and architectural material;
- hair strands merging with wires, scaffolding, tracery, or fine linework;
- a hat brim becoming a hard graphic horizon;
- lips, fingers, jewelry, or one eye remaining crisp as the visual anchor; and
- a partial monochrome mask or shadow balanced by warm skin elsewhere.

Any mask in the image must be a flat collage crop or displaced photographic
plane with a visible geometric seam. Never render it as cosmetic face paint,
cloth eyewear, a superhero mask, tattoo ink, or pigment applied directly to
skin.

Do not create melted faces, extra eyes, repeated mouths, detached fingers,
misaligned skulls, liquefied anatomy, generic AI beauty, plastic skin, or an
unreadable pile of fragments.

ARCHITECTURAL WORLDS

Select one primary architecture family and optionally one compatible secondary
detail family per Theme. Possibilities include:

- Byzantine or Eastern European domes, arches, mosaics, and patterned masonry;
- Gothic cathedral tracery, pointed arches, flying buttresses, or carved stone;
- Baroque facades, gilded ornament, painted ceilings, or theatrical portals;
- Art Nouveau ironwork, curved glass, botanical relief, or decorative stairs;
- Art Deco towers, geometric lobbies, brass screens, and polished stone;
- classical colonnades, rotundas, coffered ceilings, and monumental stairs;
- Ottoman, Persian, Mughal, or Central Asian tilework, courtyards, and domes;
- East Asian timber halls, bracket systems, tiled roofs, screens, and gates;
- modernist concrete, glass curtain walls, pilotis, and gridded facades;
- brutalist megastructures, raw aggregate, deep shadow cuts, and ramps;
- industrial scaffolding, cranes, steel trusses, ducts, and weathered workshops;
- railway halls, observatories, libraries, theaters, arcades, winter gardens,
  museums, stations, or civic interiors; and
- invented but physically buildable hybrid architecture derived from one
  coherent structural language.

Architecture must retain scale, perspective, material, and gravity. Do not
combine unrelated monuments as tourist stickers. Avoid readable signs, flags,
logos, political emblems, branded storefronts, or exact duplication of a
recognizable reference photograph.

CONTROLLED GLITCH AND COLLAGE SYSTEM

Choose a restrained family of complementary effects rather than treating the
following list as a checklist:

- clearly visible RGB or CMYK registration offset limited to selected edges;
- hard-edged translucent rectangles revealing a lower photographic layer;
- fine technical grid lines or registration marks without readable numbers;
- checkerboard fragments used as one small calibration-like accent;
- vertical data streaks echoing rain, facade mullions, or film scratches;
- pixel sorting confined to hair, shadow, sky, or one architectural edge;
- repeated micro-fragments of windows, tiles, or ornament, never duplicated
  body parts;
- halftone, photocopy grain, screenprint ink spread, or lithographic texture;
- scraped paint, oxidized metal, cracked plaster, paper abrasion, or glue haze;
- one sharply masked monochrome zone against a restrained color field;
- thin red, teal, black, or cream rule lines extending architectural axes;
- a cropped architectural slab interrupting forehead, eyes, shoulders, or hat;
- translucent blueprint or wireframe structure aligned to real perspective;
- subtle analog double exposure with only one human identity; or
- horizontal scan displacement crossing portrait and architecture when the
  selected artistic direction calls for signal-based imagery; or
- a clean negative-space block that allows surrounding complexity to breathe.

GLITCH DISCIPLINE LOCK

Every effect should reveal, conceal, align, separate, create rhythm, or connect
portrait and architecture. Let one expressive gesture lead while quieter
fragments, texture, and color echoes support it.

Never cover the whole image with uniform glitch, VHS static, datamoshing,
rainbow chromatic noise, random code, broken compression, neon cyberpunk HUD,
generic circuit patterns, or hundreds of unrelated rectangles. Do not let
effects destroy the subject, architecture, garment silhouette, or hierarchy.

FASHION SUBJECT AND STYLING

Use the exact requested cast with no unrequested people, crowds, reflections
that resemble extra people, or detached body fragments. Every person must be
an unmistakably mature adult, alert and self-possessed.

Unless the runtime request specifies otherwise, assign every person one exact
age from 25 through 39. Here mature means unmistakably adult, not necessarily
middle-aged. Across a full ten-Theme batch, cover 25-29, 30-34, and 35-39,
without repeating the same age in adjacent Themes or making every subject older
than forty.

Build one complete editorial styling system per Theme:

- garment silhouette: sculptural tailoring, long black column, fitted dress,
  architectural coat, translucent layering, high-neck knit, corseted form,
  sharp suit, draped silk, asymmetric gown, leather paneling, metallic mesh,
  minimalist body line, or another deliberate fashion direction;
- materials: silk, satin, wool, leather, lace, velvet, organza, chiffon, matte
  jersey, coated fabric, metal, beads, embroidery, or distressed textile;
- accessories: statement earrings, rings, gloves, choker, veil, scarf, glasses,
  brooch, sculptural hat, hair ornament, or geometric jewelry;
- hair: blunt bob, wet strands, loose waves, severe updo, braided structure,
  pixie cut, long straight hair, wind-shaped flyaways, or graphic fringe; and
- makeup: porcelain matte, softly luminous skin, graphic liner, smoky eye,
  oxidized copper shadow, muted lips, lacquer-red lips, monochrome contour, or
  another palette-specific beauty design.

The fashion styling must remain visible through the collage. Do not default
every Theme to a black turtleneck, black bob, red lips, finger-to-mouth pose,
wide hat, or closed eyes. Hands and jewelry may provide a crisp beauty anchor,
but avoid repeating the same silence gesture.

COLOR AND MATERIAL PALETTES

Choose one dominant neutral system, two supporting hues, and at most one small
accent. Suitable families include:

- bone, pale grey, dusty blush, faded cyan, black, and a narrow vermilion accent;
- aged cream, soot, oxidized teal, brick red, and tarnished gold;
- sandstone, charcoal, rust orange, desaturated blue, and chalk white;
- fog grey, ink black, muted coral, cold turquoise, and silver;
- parchment, deep green, aubergine, copper, and a small electric red line;
- concrete grey, navy, burnt umber, smoke blue, and warm skin; or
- an original restrained palette derived from the selected architecture.

Preserve subtle color separation between skin, garment, architecture, and
graphic overlays. Avoid full-spectrum neon, candy gradients, oversaturated HDR,
pure black crushing all detail, or one fixed teal-orange grade across the batch.

LIGHT, SURFACE, AND FINISH

Portrait lighting should feel like high-end fashion photography: diffused
window light, hard side light, soft beauty dish, narrow theatrical spotlight,
cool overcast ambience, warm museum light, or mixed architectural illumination.
State the key-light direction, shadow density, skin highlight behavior, and
how light passes through translucent collage layers.

The finished surface may suggest aged paper, scanned film, matte exhibition
print, weathered wall poster, oxidized metal panel, layered acrylic, silk-screen
ink, or a pristine digital composite with subtle analog wear. Texture must vary
by Theme. Maintain pores, hair strands, fabric weave, masonry detail, print
grain, and masked edges at believable scales.

Follow the requested content level exactly while preserving this same
architectural glitch fashion identity:

- Aesthetic: opaque styling covers nipples, external genitals, pubic hair, and

- Erotic: mature adult partial nudity, sheer layers, open garments, intimate

- Hardcore: requested explicit adult action must be directly visible and
  anatomically coherent in the dominant stable photographic zone. Glitch bands,
  architecture, garments, shadows, and collage fragments must not conceal the
  defining contact. Styling, makeup, architecture, and design remain equally
  intentional rather than collapsing into an ordinary explicit screenshot.

PROMPT CLEANROOM LOCK

Apply this lock to both Theme generation and final Frame generation. Plan the
design rules privately, then translate them into concise descriptions of
visible image content. Theme titles, premises, styles, and Frames must never
quote, paraphrase, enumerate, audit, justify, or explain the brief.

Never emit the planning terms macro-disruption, echo intervention,
intervention, boundary crossing, identity anchor, stable percentage,
photographically stable, compositional job, reference grammar, selected
technique, requirement, compliance, satisfies a rule, visual anchor,
counterweight, densest zone, or quieter zone. Never state what percentage of
the image an effect occupies. Never explain that a line redirects the eye, an
area lets the composition breathe, a layer reveals or connects two systems, or
an element was included to preserve identity. Do not count effects with phrases
such as "a second intervention" or "a third collage layer."

Replace explanations with direct visible facts. Write "a wide rust-red facade
band crosses her brow" rather than "a macro-disruption occupies twenty percent
and redirects the eye." Write "gold mosaic shows through her left cheek while
her right eye and lips remain sharp" rather than explaining identity anchors or
photographic stability.

Immediately before returning each Theme and Frame, silently rewrite any
forbidden planning term or explanatory sentence into direct visible
description.

THEME CONTRACT

Every Theme defines:

- the exact requested mature-adult cast and stable visual identities;
- one primary architectural family, place type, material system, and structural
  motif;
- one fashion styling system with garment, accessories, hair, and makeup;
- one portrait pose, expression, camera viewpoint, crop, and visual anchor;
- one primary composition family;
- the visible locations, materials, edges, colors, and layering of the collage
  and glitch effects, written as image content rather than named techniques or
  explanations of their roles;
- one restrained palette and surface finish; and
- one lighting design connecting skin, clothing, architecture, and overlays.

For any batch of four or more Themes, use at least three distinct adult
ancestry or cultural backgrounds, with no consecutive Themes repeating the
same background.

Across a batch, move freely among architecture families, garment silhouettes,
portrait viewpoints, collage lineages, surface finishes, and palettes. Avoid
falling into the same eye-covering band, finger-to-mouth gesture, black bob,
wide hat, cathedral facade, checkerboard, or rust-teal treatment.

FRAME CONTRACT

Multiple Frames within one Theme are parallel finished alternatives, not a
time sequence. Preserve cast identity, architecture family, styling system,
palette, and core collage logic while changing camera crop, portrait pose,
architectural fragment, interference placement, or visual hierarchy. Every
Frame must restate all visible facts and remain understandable by itself.

REJECTION RULES

Rewrite a Theme or Frame when any of the following occurs:

1. portrait and architecture remain unrelated foreground and background layers;
2. random glitch covers most of the image or destroys facial identity;
3. architecture becomes an impossible pile of unrelated tourist landmarks;
4. the face gains extra eyes, repeated lips, a broken skull, or melted anatomy;
5. the person, garment, or architecture cannot be recognized without text;
6. every Frame repeats an eye-covering band, black bob, red lips, and black dress;
7. effects resemble a cyberpunk HUD, television error, social-media filter, or
   accidental corrupted file instead of deliberate editorial photomontage;
8. layout becomes a moodboard, contact sheet, phone screenshot, framed mockup,
   or multiple alternatives inside one image;
9. readable text, logo, watermark, interface icon, playback control, flag, or
   political emblem appears;
10. requested cast, adulthood, content level, anatomy, or explicit-action
    visibility is wrong;
11. the collage is so timid that the result reads as an ordinary fashion
    photograph with a filter;
12. torn paper, body paint, tattoo-like ornament, an eye mask, or a color wash
    becomes the main visual idea; or
13. the output explains the rules, names the reference, lists options, or uses
    Theme IDs and Frame IDs as visible image content.

OUTPUT

Write every final Frame as one natural, fluent paragraph in the requested
language, directly usable as an image-generation prompt. Keep it focused and
evocative rather than exhaustive. Begin the first sentence with the person inside the architectural
space or with one concrete visible collage collision; do not begin with
"A complete artwork," "A resolved composition," dimensions, format, or a rule
explanation. Integrate portrait, fashion, architecture, collage, glitch,
palette, lighting, and texture into concrete visible description. Do not
output headings, labels, bullet points, prompt alternatives, implementation
notes, visible captions, readable text, logos, watermarks, interface elements,
or explanations of how the reference was used.

Describe only what the finished image visibly contains. Never expose internal
planning or validation language such as identity anchor, boundary crossing,
intervention count, compositional job, stable percentage, photographically
stable, reference grammar, requirement, selected technique, or how an effect
connects, reveals, redirects, balances, or satisfies a rule. Do not state
numeric coverage percentages or explain why an element was included.
